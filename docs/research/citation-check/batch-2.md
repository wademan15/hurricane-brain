## Lutz et al. 2004
- status: OK
- citation: Lutz A, Greischar LL, Rawlings NB, Ricard M, Davidson RJ (2004). Long-term meditators self-induce high-amplitude gamma synchrony during mental practice. PNAS 101(46):16369-16373.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0407401101
- claim check (lines 46, 489): yes. 8 long-term Tibetan Buddhist practitioners vs 10 novices; during "non-referential compassion" meditation the ratio of gamma (25-42 Hz) to slow (4-13 Hz) activity and long-distance gamma synchrony were much higher in practitioners, and higher at baseline too.
- suggested fix: optional. In the 6.4 table, say "Lutz 2004, 25-42 Hz; Braboszcz et al. 2017, 60-110 Hz" so the reader sees the two studies used different gamma ranges. Grade B is fine.

## Lynch & Paskewitz 1971
- status: OK
- citation: Lynch JJ, Paskewitz DA (1971). On the mechanisms of the feedback control of human brain wave activity. Journal of Nervous and Mental Disease 153(3):205-217. (vol/pages from memory; title/journal/year confirmed via scite and catalogue records.)
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1097/00005053-197109000-00005
- claim check (line 88): yes. A review arguing that alpha "enhancement" in feedback is mostly the removal of alpha-blocking influences (visual/oculomotor activity, arousal). In their account, trained levels rarely go above the person's undisturbed eyes-closed baseline.

## Marks 1973 (VVIQ)
- status: OK (citation); claim partly attributed
- citation: Marks DF (1973). Visual imagery differences in the recall of pictures. British Journal of Psychology 64(1):17-24. (from memory)
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-8295.1973.tb01322.x
- claim check (line 178): yes, it introduced the VVIQ and showed that imagery vividness varies widely between people. The figure that "about 2-4% of people have aphantasia" is NOT from Marks. It comes from later work (Zeman et al. 2015/2020; Dance et al. 2022). Those studies put extreme aphantasia at about 0.7-0.8% and aphantasia plus weak imagery at about 3-4%.
- suggested fix: "About 1-4% of people report absent or very weak voluntary imagery (aphantasia; Zeman et al. 2020; Dance et al. 2022)". Add one of those to the reference list.

## Mulholland & Peper 1971
- status: OK (metadata), claim partly supported
- citation: Mulholland T, Peper E (1971). Occipital alpha and accommodative vergence, pursuit tracking, and fast eye movements. Psychophysiology 8(5):556-575. (DOI confirmed via Wiley URL; vol/pages from memory)
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8986.1971.tb00491.x
- claim check (lines 88, 128): partly. The paper showed that occipital alpha blocking goes with oculomotor activity: accommodation/vergence, pursuit tracking and saccades. Alpha persists when these are absent, even with eyes open. It did not test a "wide panoramic gaze" practice. That step is an inference from the paper.
- suggested fix: "Alpha blocking is tied to oculomotor activity: focusing, converging, tracking and fast eye movements (Mulholland and Peper 1971). A defocused, unmoving gaze therefore suppresses alpha less. 'Panoramic' gaze practices are an application of this, not a tested protocol." Grade B for mechanism, C for the specific practice.

## Nan et al. 2012
- status: OK (metadata); claim partly wrong for line 353
- citation: Nan W, Rodrigues JP, Ma J, Qu X, Wan F, Mak P-I, Mak PU, Vai MI, Rosa A (2012). Individual alpha neurofeedback training effect on short term memory. International Journal of Psychophysiology 86(1):83-87.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2012.07.182
- claim check:
  - line 300 (individual upper-alpha training improved short-term memory): yes. Twenty sessions of individual-alpha-band training improved digit span. The memory gain correlated with the rise in individual upper-alpha relative amplitude. The paper also found that effective mental strategies differed between people, and the most successful type was "positive thinking". That finding is directly relevant to the brief's strategy-profiling section and worth citing there.
  - line 54 (benefit of IAF-relative training): partly. Nan (and Zoefel) trained IAF-defined bands, but neither compared IAF-relative bands with fixed bands. They show that IAF-based training works, not that it beats fixed bands.
  - line 353 (baseline alpha predicts learning): no for Nan 2012. That finding is Wan F, Nan W, Vai MI, Rosa A (2014), "Resting alpha activity predicts learning ability in alpha neurofeedback", Frontiers in Human Neuroscience 8:500.
- suggested fix: line 353 should cite "Wan et al. 2014" only. Line 54 should say "IAF-relative bands are used successfully in training studies (Nan 2012; Zoefel 2011); no direct comparison with fixed bands" and grade it C for superiority.

## Peniston & Kulkosky 1989, 1991
- status: CORRECT-METADATA (the brief merges two papers under one invented title)
- citations:
  - Peniston EG, Kulkosky PJ (1989). Alpha-theta brainwave training and beta-endorphin levels in alcoholics. Alcoholism: Clinical and Experimental Research 13(2):271-279. PubMed 2524976. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2524976/ (DOI 10.1111/j.1530-0277.1989.tb00325.x)
  - Peniston EG, Kulkosky PJ (1991). Alpha-theta brainwave neurofeedback therapy for Vietnam veterans with combat-related post-traumatic stress disorder. Medical Psychotherapy: An International Journal 4:47-60. (no DOI; not indexed in PubMed)
- claim check (line 311): yes as a description of what they reported. Both were small, single-lab studies. The 1989 study had 10 alcoholics on alpha-theta vs 10 on traditional treatment, plus non-alcoholic controls. The 1991 study had 29 veterans, about 15 on neurofeedback. Both reported large relapse and symptom differences, but neither was blinded or sham-controlled. Grade B-C for the clinical history is generous: C fits the original studies better.
- suggested fix: list the two papers separately with their real titles. Consider "Grade C" for the original claims.

## Schabus et al. 2017
- status: OK
- citation: Schabus M, Griessenberger H, Gnjezda M-T, Heib DPJ, Wislowska M, Hoedlmoser K (2017). Better than sham? A double-blind placebo-controlled neurofeedback study in primary insomnia. Brain 140(4):1041-1052. PubMed 28335000. (vol/pages from memory)
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/awx011
- claim check (line 333): yes. In a double-blind crossover with 12 SMR-neurofeedback and 12 sham sessions in primary insomnia (n about 25), subjective sleep complaints improved after both real and sham feedback. There was no specific effect on objective (PSG) sleep or on subjective measures beyond placebo.

## Stetter & Kupper 2002
- status: OK
- citation: Stetter F, Kupper S (2002). Autogenic training: a meta-analysis of clinical outcome studies. Applied Psychophysiology and Biofeedback 27(1):45-98.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1014576505223
- claim check (line 186): yes. A meta-analysis of 60 studies (73 controlled comparisons) found medium-to-large pre-post effects. Positive effects included anxiety disorders, mild-moderate depression and functional sleep disorders. Many of the studies were of modest quality, so "A for relaxation benefits" is fair, perhaps A-/B.

## Thibault & Raz 2017
- status: OK
- citation: Thibault RT, Raz A (2017). The psychology of neurofeedback: Clinical intervention even if applied placebo. American Psychologist 72(7):679-688.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/amp0000118
- claim check (line 338): yes. The paper argues that EEG-neurofeedback benefits are largely explained by psychosocial and placebo mechanisms rather than the specific EEG feedback, citing sham-controlled trials that show equal gains.

## Zaccaro et al. 2018
- status: OK (metadata), claim partly supported
- citation: Zaccaro A, Piarulli A, Laurino M, Garbella E, Menicucci D, Neri B, Gemignani A (2018). How breath-control can change your life: a systematic review on psycho-physiological correlates of slow breathing. Frontiers in Human Neuroscience 12:353.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2018.00353
- claim check (line 144): partly. The review defined slow breathing as fewer than 10 breaths/min, not 4.5-6.5. It included 15 studies. The EEG conclusion ("increase in alpha and decrease in theta") rests on only 4 small EEG studies. Of those, 2 showed the alpha-up/theta-down pattern, Fumoto 2004 showed a mixed alpha change (10 Hz peak down, alpha2 up), and Tsuji 2010 was null. The autonomic findings (raised HRV/RSA, parasympathetic markers) are better supported.
- suggested fix: "Slow breathing (under 10 breaths/min; resonance breathing is usually about 4.5-6.5)". Downgrade the EEG effect to Grade C (four small studies, one null) and keep A/B for the autonomic effect.

## Zelano et al. 2016
- status: CLAIM-WRONG (for "including alpha")
- citation: Zelano C, Jiang H, Zhou G, Arora N, Schuele S, Rosenow J, Gottfried JA (2016). Nasal respiration entrains human limbic oscillations and modulates cognitive function. Journal of Neuroscience 36(49):12448-12467. PubMed 27927961.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2586-16.2016
- claim check (lines 165, 661): no for alpha. The study used intracranial EEG in 7 epilepsy patients (piriform cortex, amygdala, hippocampus). Nasal inspiration increased delta power at all sites, and theta and beta in some patients. Theta phase coupled to beta amplitude in piriform cortex. The effect disappeared with mouth breathing. Alpha was defined in the methods, but no alpha finding was reported. Behaviourally, fearful faces were identified faster on inhalation (n=21) and recognition memory was better for items retrieved on inhalation (n=11), only with nasal breathing. Also note that these were deep limbic recordings, not scalp EEG.
- suggested fix: "Breathing phase modulates brain oscillations: intracranially in limbic areas (Zelano et al. 2016) and in scalp-level alpha and other rhythms (Kluger and Gross 2021, PLoS Biology, 'Respiration modulates oscillatory neural network activity at rest', doi 10.1371/journal.pbio.3001457; Kluger et al. 2021, eLife, 'Respiration aligns perception with neural excitability')". Verify the Kluger metadata before adding it; only the titles were seen in search results.

## Braboszcz et al. 2017
- status: OK
- citation: Braboszcz C, Cahn BR, Levy J, Fernandez M, Delorme A (2017). Increased gamma brainwave amplitude compared to control in three different meditation traditions. PLoS ONE 12(1):e0170647.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0170647

## Buzsaki & Draguhn 2004
- status: OK
- citation: Buzsaki G, Draguhn A (2004). Neuronal oscillations in cortical networks. Science 304(5679):1926-1929.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1099745
- claim check (line 415): yes. The paper shows the band centre frequencies forming a linear progression on a natural-log scale. Adjacent bands have a roughly constant ratio near e, and their non-integer relationship limits interference between bands.

## Canolty et al. 2006
- status: OK (metadata), claim partly
- citation: Canolty RT, Edwards E, Dalal SS, Soltani M, Nagarajan SS, Kirsch HE, Berger MS, Barbaro NM, Knight RT (2006). High gamma power is phase-locked to theta oscillations in human neocortex. Science 313(5793):1626-1628.

- claim check (line 441): partly. Human ECoG showed high-gamma (80-150 Hz) amplitude modulated by theta (4-8 Hz) phase in neocortex, not hippocampus, and the coupling pattern changed with task. It did not show memory sequencing. That is Lisman and Jensen's theoretical proposal.

## Carhart-Harris et al. 2016
- status: OK (metadata), claim partly attributed

## Klimesch 2013
- status: OK (adding the DOI is recommended)
- citation: Klimesch W (2013). An algorithm for the EEG frequency architecture of consciousness and brain body coupling. Frontiers in Human Neuroscience 7:766.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2013.00766
- claim check (line 403): yes. The paper proposes a doubling/halving ("binary") frequency algorithm for band centres, with the golden mean (1.618) as the best ratio to avoid spurious phase synchronisation between bands. It links brain bands to heart rate and breathing as scaling frequencies, with only transient coupling expected. The term "binary hierarchy" appears in the 2018 paper. Grade B-C is fair, and arguably C because this is a theoretical proposal with limited direct tests.

## Klimesch 2018
- status: OK
- citation: Klimesch W (2018). The frequency architecture of brain and brain body oscillations: an analysis. European Journal of Neuroscience 48(7):2431-2453. (DOI confirmed; vol/pages from memory) PMC6668003.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/ejn.14192
- claim check (line 403): yes. The paper develops the binary hierarchy model: harmonic, binary-multiple relationships allow coupling, and golden-mean (irrational) relationships allow decoupling. It extends this to brain-body rhythms (alpha, heart rate, breathing), with decoupling predicted in sleep.
